# Undercover: The Soldier Who Ate His Cigarettes (script, 19 November 2026)

**Status:** voiced and built 19 November 2026 (64.8 s take). See `wojtek.md`.
**Working title:** The Soldier Who Ate His Cigarettes
**Target length:** under 75 s (owner: under a minute, 1:15 at most).

## Script (156 words, about 66 s at Albert's pace)

Opens with a one-line hook (first 2-3 s), then the formula.

The Polish army once gave a bear his own soldier's paybook.

A sergeant fills in the papers. Name. Rank. Number.

Iran, 1942. Polish soldiers buy an orphan bear cub from a boy.

They name him Wojtek. They feed him milk from an old vodka bottle.

Wojtek grows up with the men. He drinks their beer. He even eats their cigarettes.

Then the army must sail to Italy. The ship says: no pets.

A British officer laughs. "Lads, a bear can't go to war."

So the soldiers make Wojtek a soldier. A rank, a paybook, a number.

Private Wojtek walks up the ramp.

At Monte Cassino, his company hauls heavy boxes of shells.

Wojtek watches the men lift them.

Then, his comrades said, he picked one up too.

And another. He never dropped one.

His company gets a new badge: a bear carrying a shell.

And the sergeant pulls out those papers again.

He crosses out "Private."

## Fact check

- Bought as an orphan cub from a boy near Hamadan, Iran, 1942. Sources: Wikipedia "Wojtek (bear)"; Polish History (polishhistory.pl); All That's Interesting.
- Fed condensed milk from an old vodka bottle; drank beer, ate/smoked cigarettes. Sources: Wikipedia; Medium "The Bear That Went To War"; greverse.
- British transport ship rules forbade pets; he was enlisted with rank, paybook and serial number (253). Sources: Wikipedia; War History Online; Gizmodo AU.
- Carried crates of shells at Monte Cassino, never dropping one (some debate over accuracy, so phrased "his comrades said"). Sources: Wikipedia; Farmers Journal; In Your Pocket.
- Promoted to corporal; company emblem became a bear carrying a shell. Sources: In Your Pocket; Farmers Journal; Discover Wildlife.
- Weight at Monte Cassino about 90 kg (so no "200 kg" in the script). Source: Wikipedia.
- Invented for illustration: the British officer and his line; the sergeant crossing out the form.
