# Undercover: The War Australia Lost to Birds (script, 19 November 2026)

**Status:** facts checked; waiting for the owner's go-ahead before voicing.
**Working title:** The War Australia Lost to Birds
**Target length:** under 75 s (owner: under a minute, 1:15 at most).

## Script (144 words, about 61 s at Albert's pace)

Opens with a one-line hook (first 2-3 s), then the formula.

Australia once went to war with birds. And lost.

Western Australia, 1932. Twenty thousand emus are eating the farmers' wheat.

The army sends help: one major, two soldiers, two machine guns.

A farmer watches them unpack. "Machine guns? For birds?"

Day one. The emus split into small groups and run.

Day three. A thousand emus walk up to a dam. A perfect ambush.

The gun fires. Then it jams.

So the major puts a gun on a truck.

The truck can't keep up. It's so bumpy, nobody can aim.

Newspapers called it the Great Emu War.

The final count: almost ten thousand bullets. Fewer than a thousand emus.

Ten bullets for every bird.

The major wrote that with soldiers as tough as emus, he could beat any army.

The soldiers pack up and drive away.

Behind them, the emus walk back into the wheat.

## Fact check

- 1932, Campion district, Western Australia; about 20,000 emus damaging wheat. Sources: Wikipedia "Emu War"; SOFREP; Silicon Canals.
- Major G.P.W. Meredith, two soldiers, two Lewis guns, 10,000 rounds. Sources: Wikipedia; Britannica; History Hit; Australian Geographic.
- Emus split into small groups and ran out of range. Sources: We Are The Mighty; SOFREP.
- Ambush at a dam (4 November, day three of operations) near a flock of about 1,000; gun jammed after about 12 kills. Sources: Mental Floss; Wikipedia mirror (cavac); Britannica.
- Gun mounted on a truck: too slow, ride too rough to fire. Sources: History Hit; Australian Geographic; Mental Floss.
- Final report: 986 confirmed kills with 9,860 rounds, ten per bird. Sources: Wikipedia; Mental Floss; SOFREP.
- Meredith quote about an army with the emus' "bullet-carrying capacity" (paraphrased; a 1953 retrospective attributes it to him). Sources: History Hit; scienceblog.
- Invented for illustration: the farmer and his line; "this should take a few days" is not used.
