# Undercover: Clever Hans (draft script, 18 November 2026)

**Status:** draft for the owner. Not voiced yet.
**Working title:** The Horse That Fooled the Experts

## Script (about 160 words, about 58 s)

The horse answered every maths question correctly. He couldn't count to two.

Berlin, 1904. A maths teacher asks his horse, "Three plus two?" Hans taps his hoof five times.

Crowds pack the courtyard, and the teacher never charges a penny.

A man at the back folds his arms. "It's a horse. Somebody's signalling."

So the government sends thirteen experts to catch the trick.

They send the teacher away. Strangers ask the questions. Hans still gets them right.

The experts' verdict: no trick.

Then a young psychologist tries something different. He asks a question he doesn't know the answer to.

Hans taps, and taps, and keeps on tapping.

Next, he blocks the horse's view of the person asking. Right answers drop from almost nine in ten to almost none.

Hans was never doing maths. He was reading faces: a tiny relaxing of the shoulders the moment his hoof reached the right number. Scientists still name the trap after him.

And the psychologist? Knowing exactly how it worked, he tried to stand perfectly still.

Hans read him anyway.

## Shot notes

- Opening: Hans mid-tap in a sunny courtyard, chalkboard behind with "3 + 2".
- Place-and-year beat: "1904" on a newspaper held up in the crowd.
- Doubter: the man with folded arms reappears, grinning, when Hans keeps tapping.
- Reveal: macro on a questioner's face and shoulders easing; a slow push-in on Hans's eye.
- Last frame: the psychologist rigid as a statue; Hans's hoof stops on the right number. Cut.

## Fact check

- Wilhelm von Osten, a maths teacher, exhibited Hans and never charged admission; the New York Times reported on Hans in 1904. Sources: Wikipedia "Clever Hans"; Horse Journals.
- A 13-member commission led by Carl Stumpf found no trickery; Hans answered even without von Osten present. Sources: Horse Journals; Britannica (Luebering).
- Oskar Pfungst (1907) showed Hans could not answer if the questioner did not know the answer; he kept tapping without stopping. Sources: PMC "The Clever Hans Phenomenon Revisited"; Britannica.
- 89 % correct when Hans could see the questioner, 6 % when he could not. Sources: Wikipedia; Horse Journals; Whole Dog Journal.
- The questioner tensed as the taps approached the answer, then relaxed. Sources: Whole Dog Journal; Wikipedia.
- **Still to confirm with a second full source before voicing:** that Pfungst found he gave the cues himself even when trying not to (the PMC article starts to say so; check the full text or Pfungst's book).
- Invented for illustration: the man at the back and his line; the "3 + 2" question as worded.
